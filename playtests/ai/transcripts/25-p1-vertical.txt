THE BUTTERFLY EFFECT

The machine stops screaming before you do.

You sit strapped in, sweating, your hands shaking on the armrests. It was a systems test. Nothing was
supposed to go anywhere. The panels are dark and the controls are dead.

You open the hatch expecting the concrete floor of the test bay. You get dirt. A mule cart rattles
past close enough to touch, and the driver shouts at you in Latin. You understand him.

Thank God I took that in college.

Ancient Rome. Not ruins. Alive.

The machine is dead weight. Its contacts and couplings are gold, and gold you can carry: you pry out
60 aurei worth of it. Every piece will have to go back before the machine could ever run again.
Rome pays in silver denarii. Gold has to be changed at the money changers (exchange <n> aurei).

Two things on this street could use your money, and you can afford only one:
  choose fountain  — The Subura's fountain runs brown. Clear the lead pipe and reline the basin.
  choose workshop  — Pay for a second forge and paid apprentices at the smith's by the Porta Trigemina, in exchange for a share of the business.
Your ears are still ringing. Before anything else you could walk and get your bearings: visit market, changers, forges, curia or subura (no Attention).
Type 'help' for commands.

You climb out of the machine in AD 193. The Tiber smells the same. Near the Porta Trigemina you hear

now. Prices have climbed with every cut, and soldiers grumble that their pay buys less each year.

sells them in straw, ready to fit. A fitter buys three and keeps two for the next job. You see the
other kind, too: seats cut by eye to look like the good ones, leaking back on every stroke. The men

The machine shudders. Decades pass in the dark... It carries you 40 years.

— Recognition —
You climb out again in AD 233, 40 years after your last visit. The forge by the Porta Trigemina still
smokes; the family who run it now have never heard of a partner who drew machines on wax. 'Fever
corner' is still what they call the spot where the fountain was.

— Wrongness —
The markets have grown past anything you left: new warehouses along the river, and moneychangers who
quote rates for cities you've never heard of. The coins are worse again: grey, light, barely silver
at all. Prices are quoted in bundles of coins now, not single ones.

— Personal echo —
The pestilence of 166 is an old story now, told to frighten children. Your name was never part of it.
The guild at Ostia keeps a feast for its founders now. Felix is on the list, though he founded
nothing; the speech calls him the man who taught the shops to trust each other's measures. An
apprentice tells you the foreigner Felix sponsored was really a Greek from Alexandria. A stone in the
lane under the Aventine settles an old lawsuit: the heirs of Cassianus and of Pollio share a wall and
a well by the praetor's order. The two families still don't marry.

— Discovery —
The guild you backed at Ostia is now the Ostia Grain Cartel. A handful of houses that decide who may
land grain at Ostia and at what price, and pay the magistrates to keep it so. Its head is Gaius
Sabinus Magnus, Master of the Cartel. Every fitter on the river works to one foot now, and the guild
keeps the bronze master in its hall. Fitters on the quays carry fit and no-fit plugs in a leather
roll, and buy seats from whichever shop is nearest. The pumps on the Ostia quays have seats cut to a
gauge now. The guild's fitters carry the gauges in a box, and a pump that leaks back is a joke. The
cheap seats are still sold at the market gates, and still leak. People call them Pollio's seats now,
and say the good ones were stolen from him by a foreigner. In your purse: the 10 aurei you carried,
as good as the day you left.
